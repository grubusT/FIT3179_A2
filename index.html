Australia has one of the largest natural classrooms on Earth. This is a look at who gets to learn in it, and who misses out.

By Ryan Nguyen / October 2026

Why outdoor learning matters

Ask an Australian adult about their childhood and many will describe long afternoons outside: climbing trees, riding to the creek, coming home when the streetlights came on. Ask about their own kids and the story changes.

Outdoor education tries to win some of that back. School camps, bushwalks and subjects like Outdoor and Environmental Studies teach young people to read a map, look after a place and look after each other.

Fewer than half of Australian children play outdoors most days

Each figure is one child in 100. Only 45 of them play outside, at home or away, on at least four days a week. Eight in ten parents say they want their children outdoors more.

By the teenage years, screens have won

Toddlers split their days between the backyard and the park. By 13, the balance has flipped: 70% of teenagers spend most days inside on a device, while only 21% play outside away from home.

That makes school one of the few places where many young people still get regular time in nature.

The classroom

Before asking who learns outdoors, it helps to see how much "outdoors" there is. About 22% of Australia's land is set aside as a protected area, such as a national park or nature reserve. That share changes a lot from state to state.

Tasmania protects 43% of its land. Queensland and New South Wales protect less than 10%.

Each state is shaded by the share of its land that is protected. The ACT is the standout at 56%, as much of the territory is national park and nature reserve.

A protected area is land managed mainly to conserve nature. Most are open to visitors, which makes them Australia's largest outdoor classroom.

Hover over a state for its area and number of reserves.

Most protected areas crowd the south-east, but the biggest are cared for by Traditional Owners

Every dot is a protected area of 10 km² or more, coloured by who manages it. Small state reserves and national parks crowd the south-east, close to where most people live. Across the desert and the tropical north, much of the protected land sits in Indigenous Protected Areas, which Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander groups manage under agreements with the government.

There are fewer than 150 Indigenous Protected Areas, but many are vast. Together they cover about half of all protected land, twice the area of every national park combined.

Who heads outdoors

Australians love the bush, but they don't all get out into it equally. Where you live, how old you are and your gender all shape how often you go.

The places with the most bush have the keenest bushwalkers

Circle size shows how many adults in each state went bushwalking in 2025. Darker circles mean a larger share of the state's adults.

New South Wales has the most bushwalkers, but Tasmania (28%) and the ACT (27%) have the highest share. They are also the two places with the most protected land.

Every state has its own outdoor personality

Each line is a state and each upright axis is an activity. Every axis starts at 0% at the bottom and tops out at the leading state, so the higher a line sits, the closer that state is to the top. Tasmania leads in three of the six activities. The ACT cycles the most, Western Australia surfs the most, and the Northern Territory fishes the most.

As we grow up, team sports fade and the bush takes their place

The chart ranks Australians' favourite activities in each age group. Basketball and soccer are top-six activities for 15 to 17 year olds but drop out of the top 12 by the time people reach their late 50s. Bushwalking climbs from 7th to 3rd.

Bushwalking is the one outdoor activity every age group shares

Each square shows the share of an age group that did an activity at least once in 2025. Bushwalking peaks among 35 to 44 year olds at 21%, and still reaches nearly one in ten Australians over 65. Climbing and mountain biking skew young, while sailing and fishing skew older.

Women lead on the trail. Men lead on the bike and in the boat.

Women are more likely than men to bushwalk and ride horses. Men are three to four times as likely to mountain bike or fish. The smaller activities sit in their own panel on a shorter scale, so their gaps stay visible.

Half of active adults say they do it to be in nature

Being outdoors is the fourth most common reason Australians give for exercising, named by 51% of active adults. Among 55 to 64 year olds it rises to 58%.

Outdoor education at school

For many teenagers, school is where activity happens. National surveys only count children's activities organised outside school hours, so camps and outdoor classes go unrecorded. Victoria is one of the few places where outdoor education is a full senior subject, which gives us a rare look inside.

After school ends, parks and trails become the classroom

36% of 15 to 17 year olds are active at school or uni facilities. That falls to 17% at 18 to 24 and almost nothing after that. Parks, trails and beaches are the most popular place to be active at every age.

More Victorian students study outdoor education than a decade ago

In 2014, 2,825 students took Year 12 Outdoor and Environmental Studies. By 2024 that had grown to 3,593, a rise of 27%, before falling back in 2025. The subject runs as Units 1 and 2 in Year 11 and Units 3 and 4 in Year 12.

Boys still outnumber girls in the outdoor classroom

Girls have made up between 39% and 48% of the class every year since 2014. The gap is widest in Year 11, where boys outnumber girls by roughly three to two.

Who misses out

Being close to the bush does not guarantee getting into it. Cost, time, transport and health decide who goes on the camp, and the same pattern runs through adult life.

The Australians with the least are the least active

Only 53% of adults in households earning under $40,000 a year are active every week, compared with 81% in households earning $200,000 or more. Adults who left school early, people with disability and First Nations Australians all face similar gaps. These figures cover all physical activity, not just time outdoors, but they show who is most likely to be left out.

Opening the classroom to everyone

Australians already value time in nature. Bushwalking is one of the country's favourite activities, half of active adults exercise to be outdoors, and more Victorian students are choosing to study the outdoors at school.

But fewer than half of children play outside most days, and the people with the least money, education and health are the least likely to be active. School may be the one place every child can be taken into the bush, which is why outdoor education matters.
